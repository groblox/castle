Aberwen Castle is fictional. Its plan, construction and household are modelled on Edward I's
castles in North Wales (chiefly Harlech and Conwy, with the concentric plan of Beaumaris) and on
the records of English great households. Where the evidence is thin or the scene invents, the
notes mark the statement as conjecture; those statements are listed at the end.

## 1. Taylor, King's Works in Wales

Arnold J. Taylor, *The King's Works in Wales, 1277–1330* (London: HMSO, 1974).

Supports:

- Edward I's castle-building in North Wales after 1277 and 1282–3, directed by Master James of St George.
- Harlech, Conwy and Beaumaris as the models for the plan, proportions and building chronology used here.
- Castles were garrisoned and supplied by the Crown, with stores brought in by sea and by cart.

Cited in: About; Room: outerWard; Person: carter

## 2. Wikipedia, "Harlech Castle"

"Harlech Castle", *Wikipedia* (accessed October 2026), summarising Taylor and the Cadw guidebook. <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlech_Castle>

Supports:

- Harlech was built 1282–1289 at a cost of about £8,190; James of St George was its constable 1290–1293.
- Its garrison was set at 36: a constable, 30 men including 10 crossbowmen, a chaplain, a smith, a carpenter and a mason.
- A massive twin-towered gatehouse defended by portcullises and doors, with high-status chambers above the passage and large windows to the inner ward.
- The inner ward held a chapel, kitchen, service buildings, a granary and a great hall.
- A water gate and stair to the shore let the castle be resupplied by sea, as it was from Ireland during the siege of 1294–95.

## 6. Woolgar, Great Household

C. M. Woolgar, *The Great Household in Late Medieval England* (New Haven and London: Yale University Press, 1999).

Supports:

- The household was organised in departments (kitchen, pantry, buttery, larder, scullery and others), each under its officer, and accounted for by clerks.
- The rhythm of the day: dinner, the main meal, in the late morning; a lighter supper later.
- Servants of the household were overwhelmingly male; the lower kitchen offices were filled by boys and grooms.

Cited in: About; Room: pantry; Room: hall; Person: cook; Person: scullion; Person: brewer

## 7. Woolgar, Culture of Food

C. M. Woolgar, *The Culture of Food in England, 1200–1500* (New Haven and London: Yale University Press, 2016).

Supports:

- Bread of different grades for different ranks; ale as the everyday drink and wine for the better sort.
- Many fish days in the year, met with salt and dried fish (herring, stockfish).
- Meat came into great households on the hoof or as carcasses. Wildfowl, hares and puddings (sausages of blood or offal) were all part of the diet, and butter and fat were salted and potted.
- The foods shown are all available in England c. 1290. New World foods such as potatoes, maize, tomatoes and turkeys are absent, being post-1492.

Cited in: Room: kitchen; Room: bakehouse; Room: larder

## 11. Bennett, Ale, Beer, and Brewsters

Judith M. Bennett, *Ale, Beer, and Brewsters in England: Women's Work in a Changing World, 1300–1600* (New York: OUP, 1996).

Supports:

- Medieval English ale was brewed without hops and soured within days, so it was brewed frequently.
- Hopped beer arrived in England only in the fifteenth century, chiefly from the Low Countries.
- Brewing in the countryside was largely women's work (brewsters). A woman brewing in a castle household is conjecture.

Cited in: Room: brewhouse; Mechanism: tap; Person: brewer

## 12. Bibbesworth, Le Tretiz

Supports:

## 13. Middle English Dictionary

## 19. Faggot ovens

Sally Lunn's, "The ancient faggot oven" (Bath; oven of the early 12th century), and similar heritage bakehouse demonstrations. <https://www.sallylunns.co.uk/about/the-faggot-oven/>

Supports:

- Domed ovens were heated by burning faggots inside them. The embers were then raked out with a scuffle, and the floor was cleaned with a wet mop (a mawkin or malkin).
- The mouth was closed with a stop of clay, iron or timber, and the bread baked in the stored heat. Sealing the stop with clay is attested in later practice and is assumed here.

## Anachronisms deliberately avoided

- No New World foods (potatoes, maize, tomatoes, turkeys, chillies): see Woolgar, *Culture of Food*.
- No hops or hopped beer: unhopped ale only (Bennett).
- No dog-powered turnspit, smoke jack or clockwork jack: a boy turns the spit (Caius, 1576, for the first turnspit dogs).
- No brown rats: black rats only (University of York, 2022).
- Beaumaris (begun 1295) is later than the scene; only its plan idea is borrowed.
